# Finding the Commitment

A commitment you keep is worth more than a better commitment you abandon.

This sounds like motivational advice. It is not. It is a claim about how creative work compounds, and why changing direction costs more than you think.

*[Making the Thing]({{< ref "posts/making-the-thing" >}})* says coherence requires a governing commitment: the thing you are unwilling to betray. But it assumes you have one. This essay addresses the prior question: how do you find it?

The obvious answer is: pick the right one. Research, brainstorm, validate, iterate. Find the commitment that will make the best game. This is the wrong frame. The power of commitment is not in its correctness. It is in its *stickiness*: the way it compounds decisions over time, aligns a team without constant negotiation, and converts doubt into forward motion.

Most projects do not fail because they picked the wrong commitment. They fail because they never really committed. They kept their options open. They pivoted at the first sign of difficulty. They spent so much energy debating direction that they never moved far in any direction at all.

This essay is not primarily about how to pick well. It is about understanding why commitment matters, and why the cost of changing it is almost always higher than you think.

---

## Why commitment is underestimated

Teams treat commitment like a hypothesis to be validated. "We'll try this direction and see if it works." This sounds reasonable, but it misunderstands what commitment does.

A commitment is not a prediction about what will work. It is an *agreement to stop asking certain questions*. When you commit to "the player excavates," you stop debating whether excavation is the right verb. You start asking how to make excavation feel right. The questions change. The work changes.

This is where the value comes from. Every hour spent debating the commitment is an hour not spent building under it. Every meeting re-litigating the vision is a meeting that could have solved a production problem instead. The team that commits to a B+ direction and moves will outship the team that spends six months searching for an A+ direction.

**Commitment converts doubt into motion.** When you are committed, doubt becomes a design problem: "I'm worried excavation gets repetitive—how do we add depth?" When you are uncommitted, doubt becomes an existential question: "Maybe excavation isn't the right verb at all." The first question has answers. The second question has only more questions.

**Commitment aligns without coordination.** A committed team can work in parallel. The artist knows what mood to hit. The programmer knows which systems matter. The designer knows what to cut. An uncommitted team must constantly synchronize, because no one knows which direction the others are walking.

**Commitment compounds.** Every decision made under a commitment reinforces it. The mechanics, the art, the tone, the pacing—they start to rhyme. A game built under a single commitment for two years has a coherence that cannot be faked. A game that changed direction three times has seams everywhere.

---

## The cost of changing direction

Everyone knows pivots are expensive. Few people know how expensive.

The obvious cost is rework. Art gets thrown out. Code gets refactored. Levels get redesigned. These costs are visible and painful.

The hidden cost is *alignment decay*. When you change the commitment, you do not just change the work. You change everyone's mental model of the project. Some people update cleanly. Others carry the old commitment in their bones. For months after a pivot, you find decisions that assumed the old direction: a UI that served the old loop, an enemy designed for the old pacing, a level that tested the old mastery curve.

The deepest cost is *trust erosion*. Every pivot teaches the team that commitment is provisional. They stop investing in the current direction because it might change. They hedge their work. They hold back their best ideas until they're sure this direction will stick. The team becomes risk-averse at exactly the moment you need bold execution.

This is why teams that pivot frequently underperform teams that commit stubbornly. The stubborn team makes mistakes, but they make them together, in the same direction, and they fix them. The pivoting team never makes mistakes; they just keep starting over.

## The value of stubbornness

There is a mode of working that looks like stubbornness but is actually wisdom: **refusing to reconsider the commitment when the commitment gets hard**.

Every commitment gets hard. You discover the loop doesn't quite work. The scope is larger than you thought. The market shifted. A competitor shipped something similar. A key team member leaves.

These are real problems. They require real solutions. But they almost never require changing the commitment. They require *recommitting*: doubling down, finding a way through, trusting that the direction is worth the difficulty.

The teams that make great games are almost always teams that hit a wall and kept going. They found solutions that wouldn't exist if they had pivoted. They discovered depth they wouldn't have found if they had been willing to abandon the commitment at the first sign of trouble.

But stubbornness has a dark twin: **continuing without learning**. The difference is not always obvious from the inside. Both feel like persistence. Both can be described as "grit." The difference is whether your effort buys information or leverage, or whether it buys time to avoid a decision.

---

## The rationality of stubbornness

Stubbornness is rational when it buys compounding. Games compound: a working loop tested against a clear commitment produces mechanics that rhyme, content that reinforces those mechanics, and depth that cannot be shortcut. Abandoning a commitment mid-compound throws away not just the work but the *coherence* the work was building toward. This is why persistence pays off in game development more than in many fields. The artifact is integrative. Pivoting does not just lose the days; it loses the integration.

Stubbornness is also rational when you are still learning. If each prototype teaches you something about the commitment (what serves it, what fights it, what you misunderstood) then persistence is buying information. The failures are curriculum. And stubbornness is rational when external pull is increasing: playtesters responding more strongly, market interest growing, collaborators arriving unbidden. These signals mean the commitment is entering compounding territory. Do not pivot when the evidence says continue.

Stubbornness becomes irrational when it stops buying information or leverage. This happens more often than people admit, because "I'm trying" feels like a signal but is not one. The field's actual signals are forms of *pull*: external validation that reduces your reliance on self-assessment. If prototype after prototype fails to move playtesters, if the commitment has been tested repeatedly and the feeling never arrives, persistence is not buying compounding. It is buying time to avoid facing what the evidence already says.

The hardest case is **stubbornness that preserves identity rather than serves the game**. You may have told people you are making *this* game. You may have built your self-concept around *this* commitment. Abandoning it feels like failure, so you continue not because the evidence supports continuing but because you cannot face the alternative. The tell: continuing at high burn without changing exposure or signals. If you are doing the same thing, at the same cost, despite flat results, you are paying for identity, not probability.

## Stage-gating the commitment

The right default is not "persist" or "pivot." It is **stage-gate**: commit to a sequence of experiments whose outcomes determine the next level of investment.

A stage-gate protects you from both failure modes: premature quitting before compounding can start, and prolonged overpaying after the evidence says compounding is not starting. Each gate must be defined by **leading indicators that are legible to the market**, not just to your self-concept. In game development, legible indicators are forms of pull:

| Indicator | What it measures | Why it's legible |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Playtester conversion | Do strangers who play the prototype want to play more? | External desire, not your assessment. |
| Repeat selection | Do collaborators, publishers, or platforms come back? | They chose you with their scarcest resource: attention. |
| Audience response | Is there growth not driven by one-off virality? | Sustained interest survives the novelty cliff. |
| Gatekeeper conversion | Do pitches, showcases, submissions result in advancement? | These people see dozens of projects; they're calibrated. |

These metrics matter because they change your access to future resources. A rising pull metric means the commitment is working. A flat pull metric after multiple attempts means the commitment (or your execution of it) is not producing compounding.

"Flat" does not mean one bad playtest. It means you have run multiple experiments, varied your approach, and the signal has not moved outside noise. Operationally: after 3+ distinct prototype iterations shown to 10+ fresh testers each, the conversion rate is still below baseline; after 3+ outreach cycles, stakeholders are not returning or escalating interest; after 3+ submissions with incorporated feedback, advancement rate is not improving. The threshold is not arbitrary. It is: *has effort bought information that changes the odds?* If yes, continue. If no, the signals are flat.

Before you start, define your gates explicitly. Write down what signal you need (be specific: "40% of playtesters ask when they can play again" not "positive feedback"), by when (a date or milestone), and what you will do if the signal does not arrive. A plan that includes "if this doesn't work by X, I will Y" is stronger than a plan that relies on vague resolve.

If the indicators are flat but you are not ready to abandon the commitment entirely, the rational move is to **shrink the downside with a barbell strategy**. The barbell means splitting remaining resources between two extremes: low-cost maintenance of the commitment (smaller prototypes, constrained scope, experiments that could produce pull without full production burn) and investment in adjacent paths (other projects, contract work, capabilities that remain useful if this commitment fails). The middle, continuing at full burn with flat signals, is the dominated strategy. You are paying maximum cost for a lottery ticket whose odds are not improving.

Why adjacent paths and not full exit? Because you can re-enter the commitment when signals improve. Persistence at low cost is not abandoning the dream. It is refusing to pay a dominated price for it. A commitment is "expired" only in the sense that continuing at the current burn rate is dominated by alternatives. Expiration does not mean your identity must change; it means the strategy must change. Make the criteria explicit: runway thresholds, timeline thresholds, pull metrics that must move by a date. Vague suffering is what people later mislabel as "grit." Explicit criteria let you persist intelligently.

## Moving together beats debating direction

Here is a scenario that happens constantly:

The team disagrees about the commitment. Half want direction A, half want direction B. Both directions are viable. The team spends weeks arguing, prototyping both, gathering data, holding meetings. Eventually they pick one, but the losing half is not truly convinced. The next time the project hits difficulty, the debate reopens.

Here is a better approach:

Pick one. It does not matter which one, as long as it's viable. Commit to it together. Move. If it's wrong, the game will tell you through building, not through debate. And if it's right, you'll have a six-week head start on the team that was still arguing.

**The value of everyone pulling in one direction is greater than the value of pulling in the right direction.** A team aligned on a good commitment will outperform a team fragmented over the perfect commitment. Coherence beats correctness.

This sounds counterintuitive. It feels like you should spend more time on the most important decision. But the most important decision is not which commitment to pick. It is whether to commit at all. The act of committing (fully, stubbornly, together) is what makes the commitment work.

## What makes a commitment viable

None of this means any commitment will do. A commitment must be viable: capable of supporting a game through production. Here is what viable looks like:

| Criterion | Test | Failure Mode |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Specific** (constrains decisions) | Does it tell you what to cut? | "An emotional adventure" constrains nothing. |
| **Robust** (survives production) | Can you imagine a core loop that serves it? | Beautiful but unbuildable with your team, timeline, or budget. |
| **True** (sustains motivation) | Would you regret abandoning it? | You will defend this for years; if you wouldn't regret losing it, you won't fight for it. |
| **Aligned** (travels with stakeholders) | Can you explain it to the investor, publisher, marketing lead in terms they accept? | A commitment only you believe in dies at the first gate review. |

These are not tests to run once. They are conditions to maintain. A commitment that stops being viable must be either reinforced or (rarely) replaced.

## Where commitments come from

Commitments emerge from the intersection of what you inherited, what you want, and what you can build. Most real projects are not blank canvases. They start with obligations: a pitch that got greenlit, an IP license with requirements, an investor's thesis about why this deserves funding, a sequel slot requiring continuity, a platform deal with expectations. These are not commitments; they are constraints. The work is to find a commitment that *satisfies* the constraints and *adds* the creative direction they lack.

Example: The pitch said "co-op survival horror." That is a genre tag. The commitment might be: "Two players must depend on each other in ways that create genuine tension between self-preservation and trust." This satisfies the pitch, constrains design, and gives you something worth defending.

Commitments can also emerge from a feeling you want to recreate (something from a game, a book, an experience that stayed with you), a mechanic that fascinates you (an interaction you want to explore to its limits), a refusal (a convention you want to prove unnecessary), an underserved audience (players whose needs no one else is meeting), or simply the intersection of constraints when the constraints themselves leave only one fertile space. The source matters less than the commitment's viability. A commitment born from constraint can be as powerful as one born from vision.

## Validating a candidate commitment

Before you commit resources, stress-test the candidate. The **cutting test**: imagine cutting half the game. Does the commitment tell you what to cut? If not, it is too vague. The **contradiction test**: propose a feature that violates the commitment. Does the violation feel like a betrayal? If not, the commitment may not be load-bearing. The **team test**: explain the commitment to someone else and ask them to make a design decision based on it. Do they reach the same conclusion you would? The **loop test**: can you imagine a core loop that serves this commitment? If not, the commitment may be aesthetic wrapper without mechanical substance. The **stamina test**: imagine defending this commitment for two years, after setbacks, in difficult meetings, to skeptical stakeholders. Does it still feel worth protecting?

If the candidate fails a test, strengthen it or find another. But do not spend forever searching. A good commitment committed to is worth more than a perfect commitment debated.

---

## When the commitment should change

Sometimes the game tells you the commitment is wrong. Not hard, *wrong*. The mechanics fight it. The loop resists it. The feeling never arrives no matter how you tune. The pull metrics stay flat despite varied attempts.

Recognize this as discovery.

But distinguish three things:

**Discovery** feels like revelation. "We were building toward X, but it was always about Y." The new commitment explains the past better than the old one. You feel clarity. The game taught you something.

**Drift** feels like relief. "X was hard, but now we don't have to do the hard thing." The new commitment is vaguer, easier, less constraining. You feel lighter, not clearer. This is retreat, not learning.

**Stage-gate exit** feels like data. "We ran the experiments. The signals didn't move. The evidence says this commitment is not producing compounding at an acceptable cost." This is neither revelation nor retreat. It is the plan working as designed.

If it's discovery, update the commitment and realign. You found a better commitment through building.

If it's drift, resist. The hard thing was probably the right thing.

If it's stage-gate exit, execute the plan. Move to the barbell. Shrink the burn. Preserve option value. This is not failure: it is refusing to pay a dominated price for a lottery ticket when the evidence says your odds have not improved.
